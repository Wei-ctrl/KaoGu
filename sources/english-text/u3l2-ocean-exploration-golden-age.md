# Ocean Exploration

Salt water covers roughly 71 percent of the Earth's surface, and yet, we have spent much more time exploring the Earth's mountains, forests, and deserts than studying its oceans. Scientists say that we know more about the moon than we know about our own oceans. Today, we continue to spend more money on space exploration than on ocean exploration.

Why is it that we know so little about the oceans that surround us? Perhaps it is because for centuries, people thought of the ocean as just a travel network. It was a great way to get from one place to another. Most ocean travelers stayed close to the coast. Their goal was not to explore the ocean but rather to find new trade routes for the exchange of spices and other goods.

To early sailors, the ocean was also a frightening place, full of dangerous creatures. They thought that, deep below, the ocean was a dark and lifeless place. Believing this, people had little incentive to explore the ocean depths.

Ocean exploration was also hampered by the conditions below the surface. The tremendous pressure of the water would crush an unprotected diver. Water temperature on the ocean floor was not inviting either. Vents, or openings, on the ocean floor have temperatures as high as 123 degrees Celsius.

To explore below the surface of the ocean, humans needed special equipment. Early diving suits from the late eighteenth century and early nineteenth century were not very useful. One type enclosed the diver's body in a cylinder, making it difficult to move around. A later type of diving suit replaced the large cylinder with a heavy metal helmet. Air from above the surface traveled through a tube into the helmet. These early diving suits allowed people to descend fifteen meters below the ocean surface for about an hour.

Two divers, Charles Beebe and Otis Barton, designed one of the early submersibles for deep-sea diving, the Bathysphere. It was a large, hollow, steel ball less than one and a half meters in diameter and weighing 2,150 kilograms. A long heavy chain connected the steel ball to a ship above. In 1934, Beebe and Barton descended 923 meters below the surface of the ocean in their submersible. From inside the steel ball, they were able to see extraordinary creatures. This was a great breakthrough for ocean exploration, for now, people could see the underwater world with their own eyes.

Since Beebe and Barton's record-breaking descent, improvements have been made in diving equipment, allowing people to travel deeper for longer amounts of time. Just 26 years after Beebe and Barton's half-mile descent, Jacques Piccard, an engineer from Switzerland who worked in the United States, went much farther. He and two others traveled to a depth of 11 kilometers in their own much improved submersible called the Trieste. Even at this great depth, the explorers discovered deep-sea life and new species.

The work of deep-sea explorers has given us a picture of life far below the surface. There is now greater understanding of the diversity of life in the ocean. We are now more aware of our dependence on healthy oceans. Still, less than five percent of the deep ocean has been explored, and less than one percent is protected. Sylvia Earle, one of the leading experts on oceans, says, "We're in a new century and a new millennium, and most of the planet has yet to be seen."

# The Golden Age

The average life expectancy is 72 years old. This is double what it was a hundred years ago. The majority of people around the world are living for much longer than before and staying active into old age. There are many people over the age of 70 who still work, surf, or run marathons. Plenty of people live to celebrate their one hundredth birthday, or even bigger birthdays, too. The oldest person that has ever lived was Jeanne Louise Calment. She was born in France in 1875 and lived to the age of 122 years and 164 days.

So why are people living longer? Scientists have done many studies to find out how important diet and lifestyle are in the aging process, and now at the click of a button, we can find plenty of tips. Scientists have also identified genes that might be responsible for longer lives too. In addition, in low- and middle-income countries, living standards are improving. People are living longer because improved healthcare systems ensure that fewer mothers and babies die during childbirth, and more children survive their early years. It is also easier to get treatment for infections or diseases that meant certain death for previous generations. Access to cleaner drinking water and better nutrition helps, too. But it's not just low- or middle-income countries that are noticing a difference. In high-income countries, there are advances in medicine and technology which also play a part in extending life expectancy. For example, thirty years ago in Japan, a 60-year-old woman could expect to live for another 23 years. Now, it's almost 30 years more.

More people living longer means that almost a billion people around the world are aged 60 and over, and by 2050 this number could double. In 2019, 30 percent of Japan's population consisted of people over 60, which makes it the country with the largest population of seniors. However, the younger population is shrinking because people are having fewer children. In a few decades, many other countries will see a similar situation. Europe and North America, Chile, China, Iran, South Korea, Russia, Thailand, and Vietnam can all expect to have aging populations.

While living longer means more time to spend with loved ones and do the things we enjoy, it can also present challenges to society. One of the biggest problems for a country with an aging population is a large number of older people with health problems. They need someone to take care of them, but with fewer younger people around, there might not be anyone able to help. There are also issues with housing for people who cannot live by themselves. Decades ago, older people lived with their relatives if necessary, but today, there are fewer households able to help. This might be because they don't have time, they find the task of caring for someone too hard, or it's financially impossible as the cost of living has increased. What's more, family members don't always live close together anymore. Due to industrialization, many people begin a new life hundreds of kilometers away from their families for work. Their older relatives may not be able to move with them, or their new lifestyle might not be suitable for their needs. So, where can they live safely?

Aging populations can also bring economic issues. Firstly, if there are fewer people working to make goods and provide services but more people are buying goods and using services, countries could experience high inflation. People could have to pay more taxes and wealth would grow more slowly. Secondly, in some countries, many people depend on the healthcare system to take care of them in old age. In Europe, 40 to 50 percent of the healthcare money is spent on the population over 65. With people living so much longer, most systems have to now provide long-term care, as well as treatment for age-related health problems. This is demanding on healthcare systems. Aging populations can also cause problems for pension schemes. The number of people receiving pensions is growing much faster than the number of workers that pay into them. Some people are concerned that there won't be enough money for everyone.

However, our societies usually find creative ways to deal with new situations. Some people suggest allowing more immigrants into countries with aging populations. This could help by increasing the number of people available to take care of seniors. It would also mean more workers to produce goods, provide services, and pay taxes. This solution could help stabilize countries' economies. Societies can also adapt to make communities suitable for aging populations. Sociologists agree that buildings, homes, transport, products, and services all need to be made more accessible. Slowly things are changing. For example, in 2008, the New York Department of Transport launched "Safe Streets for Seniors," which made 7,600 road intersections safer for seniors and decreased road deaths by 21 percent.
